\subsection{locating time of command}

%husk og ændre 80 sampels til det rigtige tal
Before calculating the position of the sound source, the exact sample where the command was said must be found, to avoid accidentally calculating the position of random noise. To keep track of samples, the ESP32 gives every sample an ID number, which is also sent through MQTT alongside the data.

But since Whisper does not operate in the time domain, it is not possible to link a Whisper output with an ID number in the time domain. Therefore, the first and last ID number is saved for every segment sent to Whisper, making it possible to link a Whisper segment with an array of samples.

To find the best possible sample in the array of 80 samples, Whisper is continuously given a smaller and smaller number of samples until the only detectable word is “come” which is usually around 2-10 samples. Then, the sample located in the middle of these 10 samples is chosen as the best possible sample to calculate the location of origin.




\subsection{GCC-PHAT}
GCC-PHAT (Generalized-Cross-Correlation Phase Transform) is an algorithm that calculates the TDOA (Time Delay Of Arrival) of a signal in relation to a reference signal. This is used to calculate where the signal is coming from by finding the time delay between 2 different microphones spaced by a known distance and multiplying the time delay with the speed of sound. The time delay is found by comparing the phase difference for all frequencies between two signals using cross-correlation.

In order to calculate the TDOA of a signal, the signal must be converted to frequency domain which is done by using FFT (Fast Fourier Transform). When the signal is in the frequency domain, each frequency is represented by a complex number:
\begin{equation}
    a + bi
\end{equation}
Where $a$ is the real part and $bi$ is the imaginary part. From this the amplitude and phase can be calculated:
\begin{equation}
    A = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}
\end{equation}
Where A is the amplitude, and
\begin{equation}
    \phi = \arctan2(b, a)
\end{equation}
Where $\phi$ is the phase.

The signal can be compared to a reference signal by multiplying the signal with the complex conjugate of the reference signal. This is because multiplying the reference signal with the complex conjugate of another signal gives the phase difference between the two signals, as represented by the difference in the imaginary part:

In order to calculate the TDOA of a signal, the signal must first be converted to the frequency domain. Therefore, a very precise band-pass filter can easily be applied without wasting computing time, since we are already in the frequency domain. The chosen structure of the filter is based on the telephone speech band, which is 300--3400~Hz, because that’s where human speech is most represented. To apply the filter, the frequency of every FFT bin is calculated with \( f_k = \frac{k\, f_s}{N} \) and set to zero if it is not inside the telephone speech band. Filtering out frequencies other than human speech will increase the likelihood of GCC\_PHAT calculating the TDOA of a human speaking instead of the TDOA of some noise.

\begin{equation}
    \frac{X_1(f) \cdot X_2^*(f)}{|X_1(f) \cdot X_2^*(f)|} = \frac{(A_1 \cdot e^{i \cdot \phi_1}) \cdot (A_2 \cdot e^{-i \phi_2})}{|(A_1 \cdot e^{i \cdot \phi_1}) \cdot (A_2 \cdot e^{-i \phi_2})|} = \frac{(A_1 \cdot e^{i \cdot \phi_1}) \cdot (A_2 \cdot e^{-i \phi_2})}{A_1 A_2} = e^{i (\phi_1 - \phi_2)}
\end{equation}

Where $X_1(f)$ is the reference signal, $X_2^*(f)$ is the complex conjugate of the other signal.
$A_1$ and $A_2$ are the amplitudes of the signals.
$\phi_1$ and $\phi_2$ are the phases of the signals.

The normalized cross-spectrum can then be used to calculate the TDOA by first applying an IFFT (Inverse Fast Fourier Transform) to convert the signal back to time domain. Next the difference is divided by the sample rate and then multiplied by the speed of sound (343 m/s) to get the TDOA. This process is done 3 times, because there are 3 microphones and therefore 3 different ways of comparing signals (microphone pair: 0 and 1, 0 and 2 and 1 and 2). 

Depending on what GCC-PHAT is used for a weighting can also be applied, where a weight of 1 means normalizing all frequencies to the same amplitude, and a weight of 0 means keeping the original amplitude.

The standard cross correlation is called xcorr, which only compares the magnitude of the signals which can vary a lot depending on the environment. GCC-PHAT in turn compares the phase difference between the signals by normalizing the cross-spectrum. This makes GCC-PHAT more robust to noise since the phase difference is more consistent across all frequencies in the signal compared to the magnitude. For this reason GCC-PHAT is used for sound localisation in this project.

\begin{equation}
X_1(f) \cdot X_2^*(f) = (A_1 \cdot e^{i \cdot \phi_1}) \cdot (A_2 \cdot e^{-i \phi_2}) = A_1 A_2 e^{i (\phi_1 - \phi_2)}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Triangulation}
By knowing the distance between the three microphones and the TDOA between each pair, the AOA (Angle Of Arrival) can be calculated. In practice a 3D grid is designed with a space of 10 cm between each point. For each point in the grid the expected TDOA for all three microphone pairs is calculated and compared to the measured TDOA. Then the point with the best fit is chosen as the calculated position of the sound source.
