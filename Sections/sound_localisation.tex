\section{Sound Localisation}
\label{SEC:sound_localisation}
The sound localisation is done on the processing layer. The goal of the sound localisation is to find the position of the person giving commands. For this GCC-PHAT (Generalised Cross Correlation-Phase Transform) is used to find the TDOA (Time Delay Of Arrival) between each pair of microphones.

\subsection{Word isolation procedure}\label{sec:Locating_time_of_command}

%husk og ændre 80 sampels til det rigtige tal
Before calculating the position of the sound source, the exact sample where the command was said must be found, to avoid accidentally calculating the position of random noise. The data is already timestamped from the ESP32, as seen in \cref{sec:sound_recording}, this is used to reference all of the audio data, where a bucket has 960 samples and a single stamp.

Since the Whisper model does not operate in the time domain, it is not possible to directly link a text output with an timestamp in the time domain. Therefore, the first and last timestamp is saved for every new transcription, making it possible to link a Whisper segment with an array of samples.

To narrow down the audio data, which is known to contain ``come here'', Whisper is continuously given a smaller and smaller number of samples until the only detectable word is ``come'' which is usually around 2-7 buckets at a sample rate of 16 kHz. Then, the timestamp of the bucket located in the middle of the interval is chosen as the best possible timestamp for audio data to later be used for GCC-PHAT.

This method requires heavy computing because Whisper has to decode segments 20--30 times before finding the optimal index. Therefore, the model is loaded into VRAM and processed by a GPU, which allows for parallel processing and much higher bandwidth. This is done using the CUDA driver (Compute Unified Device Architecture), which allows efficiently schedule and execute tensor operations on the GPU \cite{cuda}.

\subsection{GCC-PHAT}
GCC-PHAT is an algorithm that calculates the TDOA of one signal in relation to another signal \cite{MathWorks_gccphat}. This is used to calculate, where the signal is coming from by finding the time delay between 2 different microphones spaced by a known distance and multiplying the time delay with the speed of sound. The time delay is found by comparing the phase difference for all frequencies between two signals using cross-correlation.

An example of two raw signals received from the microphones on the robot can be seen in \cref{fig:GCC-PHAT_Raw_Signal_Example}.

    \label{fig:GCC-PHAT_Raw_Signal_Example}
\end{figure}

In order to calculate the TDOA of a signal in comparison to another, both signals must be converted to frequency domain which is done by using FFT (Fast Fourier Transform). An example of this can be seen in \cref{fig:GCC-PHAT_Frequency_Domain_Example}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{Pictures/GCC/Figure_2.png}
    \caption{Example of the frequency domain representation of two signals received from the microphones on the robot, with and without band-pass filtering.}
    \label{fig:GCC-Phat-filter-vs-no-filter}
\end{figure}

When the signal is in the frequency domain, each frequency is represented by a complex number:

\begin{equation}
    z = a + bi
\end{equation}
Where $a$ is the real part and $bi$ is the imaginary part. From this the amplitude and phase can be calculated:
\begin{equation}
    A = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}
\end{equation}
Where A is the amplitude, and
\begin{equation}
    \phi = \arctan2(b, a)
\end{equation}
Where $\phi$ is the phase.

While in the frequency domain, a perfect band-pass filter can also easily be applied, by zeroing out frequencies outside the desired range. The chosen structure of the filter is based on the telephone speech band, which is 300-3400 Hz, because that is where human speech is most represented. Filtering out frequencies other than human speech will increase the likelihood of GCC-PHAT calculating the TDOA of a human speaking instead of the TDOA of random noise. The difference between unfiltered and filtered FFT can be seen in \cref{fig:GCC-PHAT_filter_vs_no_filter}.

2 signals can be compared by finding the product of one signal with the complex conjugate of the other signal, this results in the phase differnece as represented by the difference in the imaginary part:
\begin{equation}
X_1(f) \cdot X_2^*(f) = (A_1 \cdot e^{i \cdot \phi_1}) \cdot (A_2 \cdot e^{-i \phi_2}) = A_1 A_2 e^{i (\phi_1 - \phi_2)}
\end{equation}

Where $X_1(f)$ is the reference signal, $X_2^*(f)$ is the complex conjugate of the other signal.
$A_1$ and $A_2$ are the amplitudes of the signals.
$\phi_1$ and $\phi_2$ are the phases of the signals.

This is the standard cross-spectrum, which is used in standard cross-correlation known as xcorr \cite{Bimodial_sound_source}. As seen in the formula the standard cross-correlation compares the magnitude of the signals, since the amplitudes are multiplied by the phase difference, which can vary a lot depending on the environment. In this project GCC-PHAT is instead used, because it only keeps the phase difference by normalising the cross-spectrum. This makes GCC-PHAT more robust to noise since the phase difference is more consistent across all frequencies in the signal compared to the magnitude. 

Depending on what GCC-PHAT is used for a weighting can also be applied, where a weight of 1 means normalising all frequencies to the same amplitude, and a weight of 0 means keeping the original amplitude, which is equivalent to standard cross-correlation. The weight that is most suitable for this project can be tested and found experimentally, which is done in \cref{sec:testing}.
The formula with weighting therefore becomes:

\begin{equation}
    \frac{X_1(f) \cdot X_2^*(f)}{|X_1(f) \cdot X_2^*(f)|^{\beta}} = \frac{(A_1 \cdot e^{i \cdot \phi_1}) \cdot (A_2 \cdot e^{-i \phi_2})}{|(A_1 \cdot e^{i \cdot \phi_1}) \cdot (A_2 \cdot e^{-i \phi_2})| ^{\beta}} = \frac{(A_1 \cdot e^{i \cdot \phi_1}) \cdot (A_2 \cdot e^{-i \phi_2})}{(A_1 A_2)^{\beta}} = \frac{e^{i (\phi_1 - \phi_2)}}{(A_1 A_2)^{\beta-1}}
\end{equation}

The normalised cross-spectrum can then be used to calculate the TDOA by first applying an IFFT (Inverse Fast Fourier Transform) to convert the signal back to time domain. The sample offset of the signal is then found by calculating at which sample the maximum amplitude occurs, as seen in \cref{fig:GCC-PHAT_TDOA_Example}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{Pictures/GCC/Figure_6.png}
    \caption{Example of the GCC-PHAT TDOA calculation on two signals received from the microphones on the robot, where the peak indicates the time delay between the two signals with the highest correlation.}
    \label{fig:GCC-PHAT_TDOA_Example}
\end{figure}

The sample offset is then divided by the sample rate and then multiplied by the speed of sound (343 m/s) to get the TDOA. This process is done 3 times, because there are 3 microphones and therefore 3 different pairs, corresponding to the microphone pairs: 1 and 2, 1 and 3 and 2 and 3. 

\subsection{Triangulation}
By knowing the distance between the three microphones and the TDOA between each pair, the AOA (Angle Of Arrival) can be calculated. In practice a 3D grid is designed with a space of 20 cm between each point. By having all the microphones on the xy-plane, a solution mirrored in this plane would therefore exist with a negatve z-value. These solutions are automatically not found since the grid only searches for solutions between 0 and 2 meters on the z-axis. For each point in the grid the expected TDOA for all three microphone pairs is calculated and compared to the measured TDOA. Then the point with the best fit, the point with the smallest difference, is chosen as the calculated position of the sound source. Since the TDOA values are not perfect, there will not be a point corresponds to all three TDOA pairs simultaneously. The point of sound origin is therefore estimated, by calculating the theoretical TDOA for each point in the grid and comparing it to the measured TDOA. The expected TDOA for each microphone pair can be calculated by finding the distance from the point P to each microphone and finding the difference:

\begin{equation}
\begin{align}
\Delta d^{expected}_{12} = \|P - M_2\| - \|P - M_1\| \\
\Delta d^{expected}_{13} = \|P - M_3\| - \|P - M_1\| \\
\Delta d^{expected}_{23} = \|P - M_3\| - \|P - M_2\| \\
\end{align}
\end{equation}
Here P is the point in the grid being evaluated and $M_a$ is the position of microphone a.

We also have the calculated TDOA from GCC-PHAT which can be used to find the measured distance by multiplying with the speed of sound:

\begin{equation}
\Delta d^{measured}_{ab} = \tau_{ab} \cdot c 
\end{equation}

Where $\tau$ is the measured TDOA between mic a and b and c is the speed of sound

\begin{equation}
\text{Error}(P) = (\Delta d^{expected}_{12} - \Delta d^{measured}_{12})^2 + (\Delta d^{expected}_{13} - \Delta d^{measured}_{13})^2 + (\Delta d^{expected}_{23} - \Delta d^{measured}_{23})^2
\end{equation}

The goal is to find the point P that minimises the error function. This is done by calculating the error for every point in the grid and choosing the point with the smallest error as the estimated position of the sound source. 

In total the formula for finding the position of the sound source becomes:
\begin{equation}
    \text{argmin}_{P} \sum_{(a,b)} (\|P - M_b\| - \|P - M_a\| - \Delta d^{measured}_{ab})^2
\end{equation}

In practice a 3D grid, which is a cylinder with a 15 meter radius and a 2 meter height, is designed for triangulation with a space of 20 cm between each point making the error margin a maximum of $\sqrt{10^2 + 10^2} = 14.14$ cm. However, an error of this size isn't a point of concern, since the error is relatively small. An example of the triangulation on perfect TDOA-values that match a certain point can be seen in \cref{fig:Triangulation_Example}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\linewidth]{Pictures/Triangulation_Example.png}
    \caption{Example of the triangulation algorithm used on a random point.}
    \label{fig:Triangulation_Example}
\end{figure}

This figure shows that the triangulation algorithm works as intended when given perfect TDOA values. Since this algorithm is easily understood and quick to execute on a relatively small grid, it was chosen instead of implementing a more complex algorithm. If this was the biggest error source in the system, it could either have been tweaked to be a denser grid, which would make the calculation slower, or replaced with a more advanced algorithm.
